There is a building on the corner I've passed a thousand times. I couldn't tell you what it looks like. Not the color, not the signage, not whether there's a tree in front of it. I know it exists the way you know a song exists — by the feeling it leaves, not by any specific detail you could verify.

This is how I experience most of the city. Not as a map of accurate information but as a geography of impressions, half-processed and filed away wrong. I remember the feeling of a street before I remember its name. I remember what a place smelled like before I remember what I was doing there.

## The Archive You Didn't Know You Were Building

Memory isn't storage. It's reconstruction. Every time you recall something, you're reassembling it from fragments — and the reassembly is never quite the same twice. The neuroscience on this is pretty well-established, but what it means in practice is stranger than it sounds: you have never actually remembered anything. You've only ever re-generated a plausible version of it.

> The city has a better memory than you do. It holds the shape of what happened in its streets, its buildings, its angles of shadow at certain hours. You just have feelings.

I moved to Atlanta in my early twenties and have been leaving and coming back ever since. Each return feels like encountering a revised edition of the same book — some chapters the same, some rewritten, whole sections removed and replaced with something that doesn't fit quite right. The city changed. My memory didn't track it in real time. The gap between the two is where all the interesting things happen.

I drove past a gas station last week that I was certain had been a record store. I have a specific memory of buying something there — I can feel the weight of the bag, remember the light in the window that afternoon. But the record store was gone before I ever moved here. The memory isn't mine. It's constructed from the emotional texture of other record stores, other afternoons, assembled into something that feels true because it feels exactly right.

## What the City Keeps

Cities are better at remembering than people are. They hold the physical record: the street grid that predates everyone alive, the buildings that have been three different things and still carry the bones of all of them, the neighborhoods that shifted and are now shifting again. The city doesn't forget. It just keeps revising.

What I'm trying to get at — and I'm not sure I'm getting there — is something about the specific texture of living inside a place and building an interior map of it that has almost nothing to do with accurate information. The map I carry of this city is full of wrong addresses, demolished buildings, and places that only exist on the specific afternoon I first encountered them. It's completely useless for navigation. It's the only map I actually care about.
